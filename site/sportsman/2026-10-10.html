Its 62.8 cfs and 1.40 feet comparison point is the first point of its own two-day window, the 5:45 a.m. Thursday reading (a direct pull of the Thursday dawn points puts 62.8 and 1.40 at 5:30 through 6 a.m. Thursday, and Thursday's edition recorded the same pair at 5:15), not 24 hours ago; it makes the drop look like 14.0 cfs, more than twice the true 6.1. The read string is the fetcher's band verdict, prime wading water. What 49 cfs means: on a 35-day trace pulled this morning it is the lowest reading since the 47.6 of 2:15 p.m. Sept. 11, the day the river bottomed at 44.2 before the September rains, and the 18th day of recession from the Sept. 22-23 high water, so this is late-summer base flow in the second week of October, the pocket water reduced to the plunge-pool heads and the undercuts, and the fish concentrated and spooky. The Weather Service's active list for West Virginia at 5:52 a.m. carried no rain or flood product, but NWS Charleston's Wind Advisory, issued 5:14 a.m., covers Pocahontas and Webster, where the river rises and runs, for southeast winds 20 to 30 mph with gusts to 50 from 5 p.m. Saturday to 11 a.m. Sunday, and NWS Blacksburg's product for the counties south of it names the remnants of Isaias as the cause, so the evening is the windy end of the day and the rain, if it comes, is Sunday's. At 49 cfs and clear, the river is a long-leader, small-fly problem and nothing else: a 12-foot leader to 6X or 7X, a size 18 or 20 Blue-Winged Olive or Parachute Adams over the tailouts once the sun is on the water and the olives start, a size 18 Pheasant Tail or Zebra Midge on a short dropper under the dry through the plunge-pool heads, where the fish hold in the only depth left, a size 16 soft hackle swung through the tailouts midday, and a size 12 or 14 October Caddis dry alone over the undercut banks in the last hour of light, the one big bite on the river, with the caveat that the last hour of light today comes with the wind advisory in force; with the stonefly nymphs under the rocks rather than on the water in October, a small black stonefly nymph on the dropper is the other choice through the plunge pools. Approach from downstream, stay on the gravel, and make the first cast count, because at this flow every fish in a pool sees the wade; a small olive sculpin stripped tight to the undercuts is a last-light bet only on water this thin. The morning, before the wind, is the day's window.USGS 03186500
